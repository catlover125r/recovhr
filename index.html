The Algorithm
Heart Rate Reserve
HRmax
Maximum Heart Rate
Measured, or estimated via Tanaka: 208 − (0.7 × age). More accurate than 220 − age across adults.
HRrest
Resting Heart Rate
Morning seated measurement. Two runners at the same HRmax can differ by 30 bpm here — the most individualizing input.
LTHR
Lactate Threshold HR
30-min all-out time trial, average HR of final 20 min. Or estimate as 87% × HRmax. Determines your workout type.
Age
Age
Only needed when HRmax is not directly measured. Used in the Tanaka formula to estimate maximum cardiac output.

Step 01
HRR = HRmax − HRrest
Heart rate reserve — your usable cardiac range. The foundation all thresholds are built on.
Step 02
Threshold = (HRR × f) + HRrest
f = HRR fraction for your workout type — 0.42 for VO2max, 0.50 for LT, 0.35 for speed, 0.38 for easy.
Step 03 — Drift Correction
Adjusted = Threshold + (n−1) × 1.5
From rep 3 onward. n = current rep. Accounts for +1–2 bpm/rep cardiac drift due to thermoregulation.

The Science
Why These Numbers
📈
The Recovery Curve

After a hard effort, HR follows an exponential decay. The fast phase (~30s) is parasympathetic reactivation. The slow phase (30s–3 min) is sympathetic withdrawal.

Each threshold is the point where cardiac output can support another hard ramp — not just that HR is lower, but that the system is physiologically capable of the next effort. The shaded region is the sweet spot: recovered enough to go hard, not so rested that stimulus is lost.

⚗️
Why Not % HRmax?

At the same HRmax of 188, a trained runner with resting HR 38 and a recreational runner at 68 are in very different physiological states at "65% HRmax."

Karvonen anchors recovery to the individual's usable cardiac range — not a population average. The difference can reach 21 bpm between these athletes at the same raw percentage. That's the difference between ready and not ready.

⚡
The Clock Floors

Phosphocreatine is the immediate fuel for maximal efforts. Its resynthesis has a half-life of ~30 seconds (Harris et al., 1976). 87% restored at 90 seconds.

No matter how fast HR drops, RecovHR's clock floors prevent starting a rep before the fuel is physically ready. The VO2max floor (90s) maps to 87% PCr. The speed floor (120s) maps to 93%.

📡
Cardiac Drift

In a multi-rep session, end-of-recovery HR rises 1–2 bpm per rep due to thermoregulation and progressive dehydration. A fixed threshold demands longer rest as the session progresses.

RecovHR adds 1.5 bpm per rep from rep 3 onward — raising the threshold to match where HR actually sits, preserving the same physiological readiness state on rep 8 as rep 1.

Method
Index Construction

Every parameter in RecovHR — HRR fraction, drift constant, clock floor — has a named source and a physiological rationale. No proprietary data. No black box.

1
Karvonen HRR Formula
Foundation of all thresholds. Scales to individual usable cardiac range rather than population-average %HRmax.
Karvonen et al., 1957
2
VO2max HRR Fraction (0.42)
Derived from the 4×4 Norwegian protocol's 70% HRmax active recovery upper bound, converted to HRR-based expression.
Helgerud et al., 2007 · Buchheit & Laursen, 2013
3
PCr Resynthesis Kinetics
First-order kinetics, τ ≈ 43.7s. Basis for minimum clock floors — 87% at 90s, 93% at 120s.
Harris et al., 1976 · Glaister, 2005
4
Cardiac Drift Constant (1.5 bpm/rep)
Empirical mean from middle-distance runner interval study. Applied from rep 3 onward.
Rampinini et al., 2015
5
Tanaka HRmax Estimate
208 − 0.7 × age. Lower mean absolute error than 220 − age across adult populations.
Tanaka et al., 2001
Limitations
1
HR lags for sprint work
HR peaks 30–60s after a sub-30s rep ends. Clock floor governs; HR is confirmation only.
2
Drift constant is a mean
1.5 bpm/rep from one study. Heat and dehydration can push this to 2–3 bpm/rep.
3
LTHR estimation range
87% × HRmax spans 80–92% in literature. Field test strongly preferred.
4
No RCT validation
Not yet tested in a controlled trial vs. clock-gated recovery on adaptation outcomes.
