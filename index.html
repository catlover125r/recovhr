RecovHR
Know when to go again.

Every interval workout has a window. Rest too little and you're over-stressing the system — rep quality collapses, adaptation suffers. Rest too long and you're losing potential fitness gains.

RecovHR is a heart rate reserve–based algorithm that tells you exactly when to start the next rep — calibrated to your body, your workout type, and where you are in the session.

The Problem
The Rest
Window

Clock-based rest intervals — "jog 200m," "wait 90 seconds" — don't adapt to you. A trained runner with a resting HR of 42 bpm recovers to a physiologically ready state in half the time of a recreational runner at 68 bpm. A fixed prescription systematically overtrians or undertrains one of them every single session.

Too Little Rest
Over-stressed
Heart rate is still near 80% HRmax. Phosphocreatine only ~50% restored. Blood lactate still rising. Each rep is harder than the last — not because you're working harder, but because you never recovered from the previous one. Session quality collapses by rep 4.
Too Much Rest
Undertrained
Heart rate near resting. Sympathetic nervous system fully withdrawn. The first 30–60 seconds of the next rep is wasted re-ramping toward target intensity. You're not accumulating VO2max time — you're just getting your heart started again. Training stimulus is diluted.
The Algorithm
Heart Rate
Reserve

RecovHR uses the Karvonen Heart Rate Reserve (HRR) formula — physiologically superior to raw %HRmax because it scales to the individual's usable cardiac range, not a population average.

HRmax
Maximum Heart Rate
Measured directly, or estimated: 208 − (0.7 × age). Tanaka formula — more accurate than 220 − age across adult populations.
HRrest
Resting Heart Rate
Measured seated, first thing in the morning. The single most individualizing input — two runners with the same HRmax can have 30 bpm of resting HR difference.
LTHR
Lactate Threshold Heart Rate
30-minute all-out time trial — average HR of the final 20 minutes. Or estimated as 87% × HRmax. Determines which workout type you're doing.
Age
Age
Only required if HRmax is not directly measured. Used in the Tanaka formula to estimate HRmax.
Step 1 — Heart Rate Reserve
HRR = HRmax − HRrest
Your usable cardiac range — from minimal to maximal output.
Step 2 — Recovery Threshold
Threshold = (HRR × f) + HRrest
where f is the HRR fraction for your workout type (see below).
Step 3 — Cardiac Drift Correction
Adjusted = Threshold + (n−1) × 1.5
Applied from rep 3 onward. n = current rep number. Prevents late-rep thresholds from becoming artificially difficult due to progressive cardiac drift (+1–2 bpm/rep from thermoregulation and dehydration).

The Science
Why These
Numbers
The Recovery Curve

After a hard effort, heart rate follows an exponential decay. The fast phase (first ~30s) is driven by parasympathetic reactivation. The slow phase (30s–3 min) is sympathetic withdrawal.

Each workout type's threshold corresponds to the point on this curve where the athlete's cardiac output can support another high-intensity ramp — not just that the heart is beating slower, but that it is capable of the rapid escalation the next rep demands.

The shaded region is the VO2max sweet spot — where you've recovered enough to go hard again but haven't waited so long that the training stimulus is lost.

Why Not Just
% HRmax?

At the same HRmax of 188 bpm, a highly trained runner with resting HR 38 bpm and a recreational runner at resting HR 68 bpm are in very different physiological states at "65% HRmax" (122 bpm).

The Karvonen formula corrects for this by anchoring recovery to the individual's usable cardiac range — not a population average. The difference in threshold can reach 21 bpm between these athletes at the same raw percentage.

The Clock
Floors

Phosphocreatine (PCr) is the immediate fuel for maximal efforts. Its resynthesis follows first-order kinetics with a half-life of ~30 seconds (Harris et al., 1976). 87% restored at 90 seconds. 93% at 120 seconds.

RecovHR's minimum clock floors are pinned to these milestones — the VO2max floor at 90s (87% PCr), the speed floor at 120s (93% PCr). No matter how fast heart rate drops, these floors prevent starting a rep before the fuel is ready.

Cardiac
Drift

In a 10-rep session, end-of-recovery heart rate rises 1–2 bpm per rep due to thermoregulation, progressive dehydration, and plasma volume shifts. Without correction, a fixed threshold demands longer and longer rest in late reps.

RecovHR's drift correction adds 1.5 bpm per completed rep from rep 3 onward — raising the threshold to match where HR actually sits, not where it would sit without accumulated fatigue. By rep 8, the correction is +10.5 bpm.

Method
Index
Construction

The RecovHR formula is derived from published HIIT physiology literature. Every parameter — HRR fraction, drift constant, minimum floor — has a named source and rationale. No proprietary data. No black box.

  • Karvonen HRR Formula
    Foundation of all threshold calculations. Scales to individual usable cardiac range rather than population-average %HRmax.
    Karvonen et al., 1957
  • VO2max HRR Fraction (0.42)
    Derived from the 4×4 Norwegian protocol's 70% HRmax active recovery upper bound, converted to HRR-based expression.
    Helgerud et al., 2007
    Buchheit & Laursen, 2013
  • PCr Resynthesis Kinetics
    First-order kinetics, τ ≈ 43.7s. Basis for minimum clock floors for all workout types.
    Harris et al., 1976
    Glaister, 2005
  • Cardiac Drift Constant (1.5 bpm/rep)
    Empirical mean from middle-distance runner interval study. Applied from rep 3 onward to correct progressive threshold shift.
    Rampinini et al., 2015
  • Tanaka HRmax Estimate
    208 − 0.7 × age. Lower mean absolute error than 220 − age across adult populations, used when measured HRmax unavailable.
    Tanaka et al., 2001
Limitations
1
HR lags for sprint work
HR peaks 30–60s after a sub-30s rep ends. Clock floor governs; HR is confirmation only for speed reps.
2
Drift constant is a mean
1.5 bpm/rep derived from one study. Heat and dehydration can push this to 2–3 bpm/rep.
3
LTHR estimation range
87% × HRmax spans 80–92% in literature. Field test strongly preferred for accurate workout-type classification.
4
No RCT validation
RecovHR has not been tested in a controlled trial comparing HR-gated vs. clock-gated recovery on adaptation outcomes.
